About this simulator

What it shows

A galaxy between us and a distant quasar bends the quasar's light. The source plane is what is really there: the quasar and its host galaxy, and the caustics of the lens. The image plane is what a telescope sees: several images of the quasar, and the host galaxy stretched into arcs around the lens. Each image-plane pixel is traced back through the lens equation β = θ − α(θ) to the source, so the arcs are exact for the chosen model.

Solid curves are tangential critical curves and caustics; dashed ones are radial. When the source crosses a caustic, two images appear or merge on the matching critical curve. An isothermal lens (γ = 2) has no radial caustic; the dashed curve in the source plane is then the cut, inside which a second image exists.

Using it

  • Drag in the source plane to move the source. Drag in the image plane to put an image where the pointer is.
  • Drag G to move the lens, and a perturber to move it. Shift-click the image plane to add a perturber; double-click one to remove it.
  • Hover the image plane to read convergence, shear, magnification and arrival time at that point, and to see where it comes from in the source plane. Hover the source plane to preview the images a source there would have.
  • Scroll over a plane, or use − and +, to change its field of view. Click a value to type it. Double-click a slider to reset it.
  • Arrow keys nudge the source (with Shift, in larger steps). Space plays the light curves. Ctrl/⌘ Z undoes.
  • Under Source, the host can be a smooth Sérsic profile, a spiral galaxy, a test pattern, or your own picture: drop an image file on the image plane.

The model

The main lens is an elliptical power law, κ = (3 − γ)/2 · (θE√q / R)γ−1 with R² = q²x² + y², evaluated with the series of Tessore & Metcalf (2015). θE, e1 and e2 follow lenstronomy and herculens, so parameters can be copied to and from ALPACA runs. To this are added an external shear, m = 3 and m = 4 multipoles, and any number of singular isothermal perturbers, all in the lens plane. Deflection, potential and Hessian are analytic, so magnifications and arrival times are exact rather than finite differences.

The mass sheet control applies κ → λκ + (1 − λ) and rescales the source by λ. The images, arcs and flux ratios do not change at all; the time delays, and the H0 one would infer, scale with λ (Falco, Gorenstein & Shapiro 1985). Imaging alone cannot see this, which is why stellar kinematics and the line of sight enter time-delay cosmography.

Images fainter than a magnification of 10−4 are not listed. Perturbers are in the same plane as the lens; there is no multi-plane lensing here.

Time delays and H0

Light reaches us along paths that make the arrival time τ(θ) = ½|θ − β|² − ψ(θ) stationary: minima, saddle points and maxima of the arrival-time surface. Turn on the arrival-time contours to see them. The delay between two images is Δt = (DΔt/c) Δτ, with DΔt = (1 + zd) DdDs/Dds ∝ 1/H0 (Refsdal 1964). Distances are for flat ΛCDM.

In the time-delay workspace the quasar varies as a damped random walk (Kelly, Bechtold & Siemiginowska 2009), a sinusoid, or a single transient. Every image repeats the same signal after its delay. With monitoring switched on, the curves are sampled at a cadence, in observing seasons, with photometric noise and optionally microlensing. Measure from the data estimates the delays by shifting one curve against another, and turns them into H0 using the lens model on screen. Its error bar covers photometric noise only and assumes the lens model is right.

Pinning a model makes that last assumption testable: change the slope, add a sheet or remove a satellite, refit the image positions, and read off how far H0 moves while the astrometry still agrees.

The image as observed

The spiral host is an exponential disc of old stars with a bulge, logarithmic arms of young stars, dust lanes along the arms and star-forming clumps; the quasar sits in its nucleus. Structure like this is what lets a real arc constrain the lens, because every clump has to appear in each image at the place the model predicts. A spiral also has a handedness: images with negative magnification show it wound the other way.

Lensing conserves surface brightness, so both planes share one brightness scale and one asinh stretch (Lupton et al. 2004). An instrument adds a Gaussian point-spread function, detector pixels and noise; the values are round numbers for each instrument, not a calibration. Quasar images are true point sources with the same point-spread function.
